## 3.3. Spatially written single components can be identified using a conventional classifier

Diquat, thiabendazole, and thiram were separately incorporated into the optimized ink and written as their corresponding letter patterns (Figure 3a). Although the three marks appeared similar under optical imaging, Raman mapping reconstructed each pattern using a distinct compound-selective band. Thiram was mapped at 1370 inverse centimetres, thiabendazole at 1270 inverse centimetres, and diquat at 1570 inverse centimetres. In each case, the Raman image followed the written region with limited response from the unwritten background, demonstrating simultaneous spatial patterning and molecularly selective readout.

Spectra from the written regions retained the principal vibrational features of the individual pesticides (Figure 3b). Thiram showed pronounced bands near 551 and 1364 inverse centimetres, thiabendazole was distinguished by bands near 780, 1011, and 1270 inverse centimetres, and diquat showed characteristic bands near 1174 and 1574 inverse centimetres. Comparison of six spectral regions revealed compound-specific multiband patterns rather than dependence on one isolated peak (Figure 3c).

Principal component analysis provided an unsupervised view of this chemical separation (Figure 3d). The first and second principal components explained 36.8 and 33.5 percent of the total variance, respectively, and separated the three pesticides from blank ink. A random forest classifier was then used as a conventional supervised benchmark. The single-component problem was therefore readily solvable without a specialized deep-learning architecture. This result also showed that the subsequent mixture difficulty did not arise from intrinsically indistinguishable pure spectra.

## 3.4. Concentration-dependent surface competition defines the analytical range of ternary mixtures

The analytical problem changed fundamentally when the pesticides were combined. In a mixture, the measured spectrum reflected not only the concentrations prepared in solution but also compound-dependent Raman response, adsorption, competition for enhancing sites, and local substrate gain. The surface-visible spectrum was therefore not expected to remain directly proportional to solution composition.

Single-component calibration first established that the three pesticides entered this inverse problem with different response functions (Figure 4a). The fitted concentration-response curves produced coefficients of determination of 0.916 for diquat, 0.959 for thiabendazole, and 0.985 for thiram. The corresponding limits of detection were 1.56, 1.74, and 0.37 micromolar, respectively. The substantially lower detection limit of thiram was consistent with its strong contribution to mixed spectra.

Equimolar ternary mixtures directly demonstrated the separation between prepared solution composition and surface spectral contribution (Figure 4b). At 10 micromolar per component, non-negative least-squares regression returned approximately 30 percent diquat, 23 percent thiabendazole, and 47 percent thiram. At 30 micromolar per component, the corresponding values were approximately 18, 34, and 48 percent. At 100 and 300 micromolar per component, the reconstructed spectrum was assigned entirely to thiram despite equal solution concentrations. Once the spectral evidence of diquat and thiabendazole approached zero, their original concentrations could not be recovered merely by increasing model complexity. The low-concentration range was therefore retained for model development, whereas the high-concentration series defined an out-of-domain information boundary.

Non-negative least-squares regression remained valuable because it transparently reported which pure-component signatures were visible at the surface. It did not, however, provide a general conversion from surface contribution to prepared composition. The benchmark in Figure 4c compared random forest regression, non-negative least-squares regression, a one-dimensional convolutional neural network, partial least-squares regression, and a multilayer perceptron using binary and ternary mixtures. Error increased for ternary samples across the principal methods, demonstrating the additional information burden introduced by a third competing component. The multilayer perceptron produced the lowest overall error in the current comparison, motivating its use as a nonlinear inverse model. It was introduced because the measurements demonstrated reproducible departure from linear additivity, not merely because a more flexible algorithm was available.

Within the low-concentration factorial design, condition-held-out composition errors were mapped across the complete 3, 6, 12, and 24 micromolar concentration grid (Figure 4d). The error landscape was structured rather than random, with the most difficult regions associated with low diquat abundance and combinations dominated by the more responsive pesticides. This pattern followed the compound-dependent calibration and surface response rather than indicating a uniform failure of the learning algorithm.

Component-wise concentration estimates retained monotonic relationships with the prepared values (Figure 4e). Median fold errors were 1.32 for thiram, 1.35 for thiabendazole, and 1.46 for diquat in the revised panel. The fractions within a factor of two were 85, 95, and 77 percent, respectively. The poorer diquat recovery remained consistent with its weaker mixture response. Because a factor-of-two interval is wider than the acceptance range of confirmatory residue analysis, these results support semiquantitative screening within the calibrated design rather than validated quantitative determination.

## 3.5. Physics-guided learning corrects adsorption-biased spectra and enables spatial mixture reconstruction

Figure 5 shows how the nonlinear model improves mixture interpretation at the level of individual spectra and written maps. Two representative spectra were selected to illustrate chemically different error modes (Figure 5a,b). In the first example, the prepared composition contained approximately 57.14 percent diquat, 28.57 percent thiabendazole, and 14.29 percent thiram. Direct non-negative least-squares regression overestimated thiabendazole and underestimated diquat, producing a composition error of 28.9 percent. Incorporation of the learned physical correction shifted the estimate toward the prepared composition and reduced the error to 9.5 percent.

In the second example, the prepared mixture contained approximately 16.67 percent diquat, 66.67 percent thiabendazole, and 16.67 percent thiram. The corresponding error decreased from 10.69 percent after direct linear decomposition to 4.13 percent after physics-guided learning. These examples show that the correction is not a fixed rescaling of one dominant pesticide; its magnitude and direction depend on the full measured spectrum and mixture context.

The ternary diagrams summarized this improvement across composition space (Figure 5c). Direct non-negative least-squares regression showed pronounced drift away from the prepared coordinates and strong compound-dependent recovery. The vertex recovery values were approximately 242 plus or minus 37 percent for thiram, 65 plus or minus 16 percent for thiabendazole, and 44 plus or minus 8 percent for diquat, with biases of plus 24.5, minus 10.2, and minus 14.4 percentage points, respectively. After physics-guided learning, recoveries moved to 117 plus or minus 6 percent for thiram, 129 plus or minus 9 percent for thiabendazole, and 120 plus or minus 7 percent for diquat. The corresponding biases decreased to minus 0.4, plus 2.6, and minus 2.2 percentage points. The remaining recoveries above 100 percent indicate that the inverse problem was not perfectly solved, but the marked reduction in directional bias demonstrates that the model learned a substantial portion of the reproducible surface-to-solution distortion.

Spatial maps provided a more stringent demonstration because each written location had to be assigned component-level information rather than only a map-averaged composition (Figure 5d). Direct non-negative least-squares maps were dominated by thiram and contained little visible thiabendazole or diquat contribution. After physics-guided correction, the component-resolved maps recovered spatially distributed contributions while retaining the geometry of the written “SERS” pattern. The merged image therefore encoded both the written shape and the inferred local mixture composition.

Two representative written mixture patterns and their map-integrated compositions are shown in Figure 5e. One sample remained strongly thiram dominated, with 88.18 percent assigned to thiram and minor fractions of 7.90 and 3.92 percent assigned to the other components [verify the diquat and thiabendazole order]. The second sample produced a more balanced reconstructed composition of 35.40 percent thiram, 29.47 percent thiabendazole, and 35.13 percent diquat [verify the color-to-compound correspondence]. These panels extend the mixture model from numerical regression to spatial chemical imaging.

The distinction between Figures 4 and 5 is important. Figure 4 establishes predictive performance across controlled concentration conditions. Figure 5 demonstrates the spectral correction and its application to spatially encoded mixtures. The model is therefore described as a calibrated inverse mapping within the measured domain. It cannot reconstruct a pesticide whose spectral evidence has been completely removed by surface competition, as observed in the high-concentration stress series.
