## 2.6. Linear spectral decomposition and adsorption-response calibration

Pure-component reference spectra were generated by averaging the baseline-corrected spectra within each class and normalizing the resulting templates by their Euclidean norms. Each mixture spectrum was represented as a non-negative combination of the blank, ink, diquat, thiabendazole, and thiram templates using the Lawson-Hanson non-negative least-squares algorithm implemented in the SciPy software package. A component was considered spectrally present when its relative fitted contribution exceeded 0.15 [confirm for the final analysis]. Because the fitted coefficients contain the effects of Raman response strength, local enhancement, adsorption, and competition, they were interpreted as apparent surface spectral contributions rather than direct solution concentrations.

Single-component dilution series from 0.1 to 1000 micromolar were used to characterize compound-dependent response. The response was obtained from marker bands within plus or minus 8 inverse centimetres of 1572 and 1176 inverse centimetres for diquat, 1010 and 1272 inverse centimetres for thiabendazole, and 1368 and 550 inverse centimetres for thiram. The concentration dependence was fitted using

\[
B(C)=\frac{gAKC}{1+KC},
\]

where \(B\) is the measured response, \(gA\) is an empirical amplitude term, \(K\) is an apparent adsorption parameter, and \(C\) is the solution concentration. A heterogeneous adsorption exponent was introduced for [diquat or the finalized compound set] when required by the calibration data. The limit of detection was calculated as 3.3 times the between-map standard deviation of seven blank maps divided by the low-concentration slope. The limit of quantification was calculated using a multiplier of ten.

## 2.7. Physics-guided nonlinear model for mixture interpretation

The nonlinear mixture model combined spectral gating, calibration-informed pretraining, and supervised learning from measured mixtures. Non-negative least-squares regression was first used to exclude background pixels and retain spectra containing sufficient analyte evidence. The retained baseline-corrected spectra were transformed using the logarithm of one plus the raw intensity without Euclidean normalization so that both spectral shape and response magnitude were preserved.

The multilayer perceptron consisted of a fully connected layer with 256 hidden units, batch normalization, a rectified linear activation, dropout with a probability of 0.15, a second fully connected layer with 64 hidden units, a second rectified linear activation, and a three-unit output layer. A softmax transformation constrained the predicted fractions to be non-negative and to sum to unity. The model was optimized using Adam with a learning rate of 0.0003, a weight-decay coefficient of 0.001, and a fixed training duration of 300 epochs. The loss was a weighted absolute composition error, with larger weights assigned to minor components to reduce systematic suppression of low-abundance pesticides.

Before supervised training, synthetic mixtures generated from the single-component calibration functions were used for physics-guided pretraining. The pretrained network was subsequently refined using measured mixtures with known solution compositions. Predictive evaluation was performed by leaving out one complete concentration condition at a time. All replicate maps and all pixel spectra associated with the held-out condition were excluded from model fitting. Model selection, preprocessing selection, and training-duration selection were completed without access to the held-out condition.

## 2.8. Model interpretation and statistical analysis

The spectral basis of each model output was examined using integrated-gradient attribution, spectral-window permutation, and targeted band ablation. Integrated gradients quantified the contribution of each Raman shift to the predicted fraction of each pesticide. For permutation analysis, consecutive 60-inverse-centimetre spectral windows were disrupted and the resulting increase in composition error was recorded. For targeted ablation, regions surrounding independently identified pesticide bands were removed and the relative change in the corresponding prediction was calculated. The explanatory model fitted to the complete data set was used only to visualize learned associations and not to estimate predictive performance. Final attribution statistics will be calculated across condition-held-out folds and independent random seeds, with shuffled-label and shifted-band analyses serving as negative controls.

Composition error was defined as one half of the sum of the absolute differences between the predicted and prepared component percentages. Concentration performance was summarized using the median fold error and the fractions of predictions within factors of two and three. Confidence intervals were calculated by resampling independent maps or independently prepared substrates rather than individual pixels. # 3. Results and Discussion

## 3.1. Spatially encoded single-component sensing using surface-enhanced Raman scattering ink

We first evaluated whether the surface-enhanced Raman scattering ink could preserve the spatial geometry of a written pattern while retaining compound-specific molecular information. Diquat, thiabendazole, and thiram were separately incorporated into the ink and written as their corresponding letter patterns (Figure 3a). Although the three marks were visually similar under optical imaging, Raman mapping reconstructed each pattern using a different compound-selective band. The thiram pattern was visualized at 1370 inverse centimetres, the thiabendazole pattern at 1270 inverse centimetres, and the diquat pattern at 1570 inverse centimetres. In each case, the Raman image followed the written region and showed limited response from the unwritten background, demonstrating that the ink provides both spatial patterning and molecularly selective readout.

The spectra acquired from the written regions retained the principal vibrational features of the individual pesticides (Figure 3b). Thiram exhibited pronounced bands near 551 and 1364 inverse centimetres, thiabendazole was distinguished by responses near 780, 1011, and 1270 inverse centimetres, and diquat showed characteristic bands near 1174 and 1574 inverse centimetres. Comparison of six discriminating spectral regions further revealed a compound-specific multiband pattern rather than dependence on a single peak (Figure 3c). This distributed spectral information is advantageous because classification remains possible even when the intensity of one band fluctuates across the substrate.

Principal component analysis of the pixel spectra provided an unsupervised view of this chemical separation (Figure 3d). The first and second principal components explained 36.8 and 33.5 percent of the total variance, respectively, and separated the three pesticides from the blank ink. Thiram and the blank formed particularly distinct clusters, while diquat and thiabendazole occupied neighboring but distinguishable regions. A random forest classifier was then used as a conventional supervised benchmark. These results establish that the single-component problem is readily solvable without a specialized deep-learning architecture. More importantly, they show that the difficulty encountered in subsequent mixture analysis does not originate from intrinsically indistinguishable pure spectra.

## 3.2. Competitive surface response creates a nonlinear mixture problem

The analytical problem changed fundamentally when the three pesticides were combined. For an isolated compound, the presence of characteristic bands was sufficient for identification. In a mixture, the measured spectrum reflected not only the concentrations prepared in solution but also compound-dependent Raman response, adsorption affinity, competition for enhancing sites, and local substrate gain. The spectral contribution observed at the surface was therefore not expected to remain directly proportional to the solution composition.

This distortion was evident across the ternary composition space (Figure 4a). Recovery and bias varied with composition and differed among the three pesticide vertices, indicating that a single global response correction could not account for the observed behavior. Independent concentration-response measurements yielded limits of detection of 1.56 micromolar for diquat, 1.74 micromolar for thiabendazole, and 0.37 micromolar for thiram (Figure 4b). The substantially lower detection limit of thiram was consistent with its strong contribution to mixed spectra.

Equimolar ternary mixtures provided a direct demonstration of the discrepancy between solution composition and surface spectral contribution (Figure 4c). At 10 micromolar per component, linear decomposition returned approximately 30 percent diquat, 23 percent thiabendazole, and 47 percent thiram. At 33 micromolar per component, the corresponding values were approximately 18, 34, and 48 percent. When the concentration was increased to 100 or 333 micromolar per component, the reconstructed spectrum was assigned almost entirely to thiram, despite the equal concentrations of all three pesticides in solution. Thus, the surface measurement did not preserve the equimolar composition at high loading. Once the spectral evidence of diquat and thiabendazole approached zero, their original concentrations could not be recovered by increasing the complexity of the decomposition algorithm.

These observations define a practical information boundary. The low-concentration factorial design spanning 3 to 24 micromolar per component was selected for composition learning because all three compounds remained spectrally represented over most of this range. The high-concentration series was retained as a stress test that identified surface domination and out-of-domain behavior. Predictions from this regime were not interpreted quantitatively.

## 3.3. Linear spectral decomposition reports surface visibility but not prepared composition

Non-negative least-squares regression was initially selected because it is transparent and physically constrained. The measured spectrum is reconstructed as a non-negative sum of pure-component templates, allowing the visible contribution of each pesticide to be inspected together with the residual spectrum. This approach worked well for determining which pure spectral signatures were present at the surface. It did not, however, provide a general inverse transformation from surface signal to solution composition.

The reason is that each fitted coefficient combines several latent quantities. A coefficient increases with local enhancement and intrinsic Raman response, but also depends on the fraction of enhancing sites occupied by that molecule. Under competitive adsorption, this surface fraction changes with the concentrations and adsorption strengths of all components. Consequently, identical solution fractions can produce different spectral fractions, while a strongly adsorbing or strongly scattering component can suppress the apparent contributions of the others. Linear decomposition therefore answers the question “what is visible at the surface?” rather than “what fraction was prepared in solution?”

The comparison in Figure 4d further showed that the challenge increased from binary to ternary mixtures across random forest classification, non-negative least-squares regression, a one-dimensional convolutional neural network, partial least-squares discriminant analysis, and a multilayer perceptron. The larger ternary errors indicate that the third competing compound introduces a genuine information burden rather than a method-specific failure. The multilayer perceptron produced the lowest or among the lowest held-out errors in the finalized comparison [insert harmonized values], motivating its use as a nonlinear inverse model. This model was not introduced merely because it was more flexible. It was introduced because the experiments had already demonstrated a reproducible departure from linear additivity.

## 3.4. Physics-guided nonlinear learning recovers ternary composition within the calibrated range

To recover the prepared composition, we constructed a hybrid analysis that retained the interpretability of linear spectral evidence while learning the nonlinear transformation imposed by the sensing interface. Non-negative least-squares regression was used as an initial gate to remove background spectra and prevent the neural model from assigning pesticides to pixels lacking measurable analyte evidence. Calibration-derived response functions were then used to generate physically informed synthetic mixtures for pretraining. Finally, labeled experimental mixtures were used to refine the relationship between the full measured spectrum and the known solution fractions.

The resulting multilayer perceptron reproduced the overall composition landscape across the low-concentration simplex (Figure 4e). The current panel reports recoveries of 129 plus or minus 9 percent for thiabendazole, 117 plus or minus 6 percent for thiram, and 120 plus or minus 7 percent for diquat [verify the component order against the final artwork]. Unlike the high-concentration linear decomposition, the learned predictions did not collapse completely to the strongest surface component within the calibrated design. The corresponding error maps showed that performance remained composition dependent (Figure 4f). Conditions containing low diquat concentrations were generally more difficult, consistent with the weaker diquat response and its higher quantification limit.

Across the complete low-concentration data set, current analysis versions yield a mean composition error of approximately 12 to 14 percent under condition-held-out validation. One exact value should be reported after the final Figure 4 table is locked, because earlier analyses used different numbers of conditions and different spectral aggregation units. The decisive comparison must use identical conditions, preprocessing, and validation folds for every method. Under that restriction, the central conclusion is that physics-guided nonlinear learning recovers solution composition more accurately than direct linear surface decomposition within the calibrated range.

## 3.5. Component-wise concentration estimates support semiquantitative screening

The recovered composition was next translated into component-wise concentration estimates (Figure 4g). At concentrations of 6 micromolar or higher, the median fold errors were 1.67 for diquat, 1.36 for thiabendazole, and 1.33 for thiram. The fractions of measurements predicted within a factor of two were 65 percent for diquat, 87 percent for thiabendazole, and 89 percent for thiram. The corresponding fractions within a factor of three were 92, 98, and 98 percent. The cumulative fold-error distributions in Figure 4h show that most of the remaining large errors arose near the lower boundary of the design.

This concentration dependence was consistent with the independent calibration results. The limits of quantification were 4.72 micromolar for diquat, 5.26 micromolar for thiabendazole, and 1.13 micromolar for thiram. At 3 micromolar, only 19 percent of diquat estimates and 50 percent of thiabendazole estimates were within a factor of two, whereas thiram reached 81 percent. A reporting threshold of 6 micromolar therefore represents the first tested level above the limits of quantification of all three compounds. Values below the component-specific threshold should be reported as below the quantifiable range rather than as precise concentrations.

The model is consequently described as enabling semiquantitative screening, not validated quantitative residue analysis. A factor-of-two interval is substantially wider than the recovery range required for confirmatory analytical methods. The present advance lies instead in extracting useful component-level information from a competitively adsorbed and spatially heterogeneous Raman signal that cannot be interpreted by direct peak intensity or linear coefficients alone.

## 3.6. Explainable artificial intelligence provides a chemical consistency check for the nonlinear model

The increased flexibility of the multilayer perceptron requires verification that its predictions remain connected to physically and spectroscopically meaningful information. We therefore examined the trained model using integrated-gradient attribution, spectral-window permutation, and targeted band ablation. These complementary analyses were designed to determine whether the model relied on the same compound-associated Raman regions identified in the single-component experiments and whether the learned response was consistent with the adsorption-informed calibration used during pretraining.

The appropriate interpretation of these analyses is model consistency rather than causal mechanism. Attribution near known pesticide bands would indicate that the network uses chemically plausible spectral evidence, whereas strong attribution in featureless baseline regions would suggest sensitivity to acquisition artifacts. Likewise, a decrease in the corresponding pesticide output after removal of its characteristic bands would support a compound-specific decision pathway. In the current full-data explanatory fit, targeted ablation changed the corresponding outputs by approximately 1 to 5 percent, while individual band-permutation effects were small. These preliminary results do not yet support a strong mechanistic claim. Attribution stability across held-out conditions and random seeds, together with shuffled-label and shifted-band negative controls, will therefore be included before the final interpretation panel is fixed.

Taken together, Figures 3 and 4 describe a deliberate progression in analytical complexity. Single-component patterns can be mapped and classified using well-resolved Raman fingerprints and a conventional random forest model. Mixtures are more demanding because competitive adsorption distorts the relationship between solution concentration and surface-visible spectrum. Linear decomposition remains valuable for identifying the spectral evidence that survives at the surface, but nonlinear learning is required to infer the prepared composition within a calibrated domain. By incorporating adsorption-response calibration and band-level explanation, the final model extends from classification to mixture interpretation without treating the neural network as an unconstrained black box.
