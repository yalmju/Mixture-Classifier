### 2.10. Linear Spectral Decomposition and Calibration

Pure-component reference spectra were baseline corrected, averaged by class, and normalized by their Euclidean norms. Each mixture spectrum was represented as a non-negative combination of blank, ink, diquat, thiabendazole, and thiram templates using the Lawson-Hanson non-negative least-squares algorithm implemented in the SciPy software package. A component was considered surface-visible when its relative fitted contribution exceeded 0.15 [confirm for the final analysis]. Because fitted coefficients contain compound-dependent Raman response, local enhancement, adsorption, and competition, they were interpreted as apparent surface spectral contributions rather than direct solution concentrations.

Single-component dilution series were fitted using the selected adsorption-response function. The limit of detection was calculated as 3.3 times the between-map standard deviation of seven blank maps divided by the low-concentration slope. The limit of quantification was calculated using a multiplier of ten. The same response functions were subsequently used to generate physics-informed synthetic mixtures for model pretraining.

### 2.11. Physics-Guided Nonlinear Mixture Model

The nonlinear model combined spectral gating, calibration-informed pretraining, and supervised learning from measured mixtures. Non-negative least-squares regression first excluded background spectra. The retained baseline-corrected spectra were transformed using the logarithm of one plus raw intensity without Euclidean normalization, preserving spectral shape and response magnitude.

The multilayer perceptron comprised a fully connected layer with 256 hidden units, batch normalization, a rectified linear activation, dropout with probability 0.15, a second fully connected layer with 64 hidden units, a second rectified linear activation, and a three-unit output layer. A softmax transformation constrained predicted fractions to be non-negative and sum to unity. Optimization used Adam with a learning rate of 0.0003, weight decay of 0.001, and a fixed training duration of 300 epochs. A weighted absolute composition loss assigned greater weight to minor components.

Before supervised refinement, synthetic mixtures generated from single-component calibration functions were used for physics-guided pretraining. Predictive evaluation left out one complete concentration condition at a time. Every map and pixel associated with the held-out condition was excluded from model fitting, preprocessing selection, and training-duration selection.

For spatial mixture reconstruction, the trained model was applied to analyte-positive pixel spectra selected by the non-negative least-squares gate. The three component fractions were predicted for each retained pixel and displayed using fixed compound colors. Map-integrated composition was obtained by [state mean, median, or weighted aggregation] across accepted pixels. ## 3. Results and Discussion

### 3.1. Interfacial Reduction Produces Protruded Gold Architectures on Cellulose Nanofibers

The gold-cellulose nanofiber composite was designed to combine plasmonic enhancement and ink-forming behavior within one material. Cellulose nanofibers first acted as a high-area scaffold that concentrated gold ions at oxygen-rich surface groups. Addition of hydroxylamine then initiated interfacial reduction, nucleation, and outward growth of gold domains (Figure 1a). Continued growth generated clustered structures with nanoscale protrusions rather than only isolated spherical particles. These protrusions and narrow junctions between neighboring domains were expected to increase the density of high-curvature regions and electromagnetically coupled gaps.

Scanning electron microscopy showed that gold morphology varied with hydroxylamine concentration (Figure 1b). At [lowest concentration], the product showed [insert observation]. Increasing the concentration to [optimized concentration] produced [insert quantified change in domain density, protrusion development, or uniformity]. At [highest concentration], [insert observation]. Optical photographs and extinction spectra changed concurrently with this structural evolution (Figure 1c). The selected formulation displayed a broad optical response extending toward the 785-nanometre excitation wavelength. The final analysis should report peak positions, spectral widths, and replicate variability rather than rely on visible color change alone.

X-ray diffraction confirmed crystalline face-centered-cubic gold through reflections corresponding to the (111), (200), (220), (311), and (222) planes (Figure 1d). Transmission electron microscopy and elemental mapping further showed gold-rich protruded domains associated with the carbon- and oxygen-containing nanofiber framework (Figure 1e). Nitrogen mapping requires cautious interpretation because residual nitrogen may originate from hydroxylamine-derived species, preparation artifacts, or background.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy will test the chemical sequence proposed in Figure 1a. High-resolution gold 4f spectra will determine whether metallic gold dominates after reduction and washing, while carbon 1s and oxygen 1s spectra will determine whether gold loading alters the electronic environment of the cellulose surface. Complementary infrared spectra will determine whether the polysaccharide backbone remains intact and whether hydroxyl and carbon-oxygen environments change after loading and reduction. These measurements can support interfacial association and modification of hydrogen bonding, but they will not be used alone to claim a specific covalent gold-oxygen bond.

Together, the available structural data support a material in which cellulose nanofibers spatially organize gold reduction and stabilize a protruded plasmonic architecture. Final selection of the optimized condition will be based on reproducible structural, optical, rheological, and Raman criteria.

### 3.2. Recoverable Ink Mechanics Couple Direct Writing with Raman Enhancement

The cellulose nanofiber network must satisfy two opposing mechanical requirements. The ink should flow under shear generated during writing but should rebuild after deposition to suppress lateral spreading and preserve pattern resolution. Steady-shear measurements will determine whether the optimized ink exhibits decreasing viscosity with increasing shear rate. This behavior will establish shear-thinning, while time-dependent structural recovery after high shear will be required before the material is described as thixotropic.

Oscillatory measurements will define the linear viscoelastic region and compare storage and loss moduli at rest. A storage modulus exceeding the loss modulus within the relevant low-strain range would support the presence of a weak solid-like network. A three-interval test will then quantify disruption under high shear and recovery after returning to low shear. Recovery fraction and recovery time will be compared across pristine cellulose nanofiber ink, nonoptimized composites, and the selected formulation. Direct measurements of line width, spreading, edge roughness, and drying-induced dimensional change will connect rheological properties with actual writing performance.

Electromagnetic simulation provided a complementary optical rationale for the protruded structure (Figure 2). Under 785-nanometre excitation, enhanced local fields were concentrated at protrusion tips and narrow junctions between neighboring domains. Because the experimental ink contains distributions of shapes, orientations, and gap sizes, these calculations are interpreted as identifying plausible hotspot locations rather than predicting absolute ensemble enhancement.

The optimized material produced concentration-dependent Raman spectra of malachite green and a linear response over the tested working range (Figure 2). Spatial mapping yielded a within-map relative standard deviation of 10.8 percent in the current panel. Before publication, this value will be supplemented with the number of mapped points, number of independently prepared substrates, and between-substrate variability. The combined data are intended to support a dual-function material claim: the cellulose nanofiber network provides shear-enabled processability and post-writing recovery, whereas the protruded gold structures provide local electromagnetic enhancement.

### 3.3. Spatially Written Single Components Can Be Identified Using a Conventional Classifier

Diquat, thiabendazole, and thiram were separately incorporated into the optimized ink and written as their corresponding letter patterns (Figure 3a). Although the three marks appeared similar under optical imaging, Raman mapping reconstructed each pattern using a distinct compound-selective band. Thiram was mapped at 1370 inverse centimetres, thiabendazole at 1270 inverse centimetres, and diquat at 1570 inverse centimetres. In each case, the Raman image followed the written region with limited response from unwritten background, demonstrating simultaneous spatial patterning and molecularly selective readout.

Spectra from the written regions retained the principal vibrational features of the individual pesticides (Figure 3b). Thiram showed pronounced bands near 551 and 1364 inverse centimetres, thiabendazole was distinguished by bands near 780, 1011, and 1270 inverse centimetres, and diquat showed characteristic bands near 1174 and 1574 inverse centimetres. Comparison of six spectral regions revealed compound-specific multiband patterns rather than dependence on one isolated peak (Figure 3c).

Principal component analysis provided an unsupervised view of chemical separation (Figure 3d). The first and second principal components explained 36.8 and 33.5 percent of total variance, respectively, and separated the three pesticides from blank ink. A random forest classifier was then used as a conventional supervised benchmark. With [spatially or batch-separated] held-out data, the model achieved an accuracy of [value] and a macro-averaged F1 score of [value]. The single-component problem was therefore readily solvable without a specialized deep-learning architecture. This result also showed that subsequent mixture difficulty did not arise from intrinsically indistinguishable pure spectra.

### 3.4. Concentration-Dependent Surface Competition Defines the Analytical Range of Ternary Mixtures

The analytical problem changed fundamentally when the pesticides were combined. In a mixture, the measured spectrum reflected not only concentrations prepared in solution but also compound-dependent Raman response, adsorption, competition for enhancing sites, and local substrate gain. The surface-visible spectrum was therefore not expected to remain directly proportional to solution composition.

Single-component calibration first established that the three pesticides entered this inverse problem with different response functions (Figure 4a). Fitted concentration-response curves produced coefficients of determination of 0.916 for diquat, 0.959 for thiabendazole, and 0.985 for thiram. The corresponding limits of detection were 1.56, 1.74, and 0.37 micromolar, respectively. The substantially lower detection limit of thiram was consistent with its strong contribution to mixed spectra.

Equimolar ternary mixtures directly demonstrated separation between prepared solution composition and surface spectral contribution (Figure 4b). At 10 micromolar per component, non-negative least-squares regression returned approximately 30 percent diquat, 23 percent thiabendazole, and 47 percent thiram. At [confirm 30 or 33] micromolar per component, corresponding values were approximately 18, 34, and 48 percent. At [confirm 100 and 300 or 333] micromolar per component, the reconstructed spectrum was assigned entirely to thiram despite equal solution concentrations. Once spectral evidence of diquat and thiabendazole approached zero, their original concentrations could not be recovered merely by increasing model complexity. The low-concentration range was therefore retained for model development, whereas the high-concentration series defined an out-of-domain information boundary.

Non-negative least-squares regression remained valuable because it transparently reported which pure-component signatures were visible at the surface. It did not, however, provide a general conversion from surface contribution to prepared composition. Figure 4c compared random forest regression, non-negative least-squares regression, a one-dimensional convolutional neural network, partial least-squares [confirm method], and a multilayer perceptron using binary and ternary mixtures. Error increased for ternary samples across the principal methods, demonstrating the additional information burden introduced by a third competing component. The multilayer perceptron produced the lowest overall error in the current comparison, motivating its use as a nonlinear inverse model. It was introduced because the measurements demonstrated reproducible departure from linear additivity, not merely because a more flexible algorithm was available.

Within the low-concentration factorial design, condition-held-out composition errors were mapped across the complete 3, 6, 12, and 24 micromolar concentration grid (Figure 4d). The error landscape was structured rather than random, with the most difficult regions associated with low diquat abundance and combinations dominated by more responsive pesticides. This pattern followed compound-dependent calibration and surface response rather than indicating uniform model failure.

Component-wise concentration estimates retained monotonic relationships with prepared values (Figure 4e). Median fold errors were 1.32 for thiram, 1.35 for thiabendazole, and 1.46 for diquat. Fractions within a factor of two were 85, 95, and 77 percent, respectively. The poorer diquat recovery remained consistent with its weaker mixture response. Because a factor-of-two interval is wider than the acceptance range of confirmatory residue analysis, these results support semiquantitative screening within the calibrated design rather than validated quantitative determination.

### 3.5. Physics-Guided Learning Corrects Adsorption-Biased Spectra and Enables Spatial Mixture Reconstruction

Figure 5 shows how the nonlinear model improves mixture interpretation at the level of individual spectra and written maps. Two representative spectra were selected to illustrate chemically different error modes (Figure 5a,b). In the first example, the prepared composition contained approximately 57.14 percent diquat, 28.57 percent thiabendazole, and 14.29 percent thiram. Direct non-negative least-squares regression overestimated thiabendazole and underestimated diquat, producing a composition error of 28.9 percent. Incorporation of the learned physical correction shifted the estimate toward the prepared composition and reduced the error to 9.5 percent.

In the second example, the prepared mixture contained approximately 16.67 percent diquat, 66.67 percent thiabendazole, and 16.67 percent thiram. The corresponding error decreased from 10.69 percent after direct linear decomposition to 4.13 percent after physics-guided learning. These examples show that the correction is not a fixed rescaling of one dominant pesticide; its magnitude and direction depend on the full measured spectrum and mixture context.

The ternary diagrams summarized this improvement across composition space (Figure 5c). Direct non-negative least-squares regression showed pronounced drift away from prepared coordinates and strong compound-dependent recovery. Vertex recovery values were approximately 242 plus or minus 37 percent for thiram, 65 plus or minus 16 percent for thiabendazole, and 44 plus or minus 8 percent for diquat, with biases of plus 24.5, minus 10.2, and minus 14.4 percentage points, respectively. After physics-guided learning, recoveries moved to 117 plus or minus 6 percent for thiram, 129 plus or minus 9 percent for thiabendazole, and 120 plus or minus 7 percent for diquat. Corresponding biases decreased to minus 0.4, plus 2.6, and minus 2.2 percentage points. Remaining recoveries above 100 percent indicate that the inverse problem was not perfectly solved, but the marked reduction in directional bias demonstrates that the model learned a substantial portion of the reproducible surface-to-solution distortion.

Spatial maps provided a more stringent demonstration because each written location had to be assigned component-level information rather than only a map-averaged composition (Figure 5d). Direct non-negative least-squares maps were dominated by thiram and contained little visible thiabendazole or diquat contribution. After physics-guided correction, component-resolved maps recovered spatially distributed contributions while retaining the geometry of the written “SERS” pattern. The merged image therefore encoded both written shape and inferred local mixture composition.

Two representative written mixture patterns and their map-integrated compositions are shown in Figure 5e. One sample remained strongly thiram dominated, with 88.18 percent assigned to thiram and minor fractions of 7.90 and 3.92 percent assigned to the other components [verify diquat and thiabendazole order]. The second sample produced a more balanced reconstructed composition of 35.40 percent thiram, 29.47 percent thiabendazole, and 35.13 percent diquat [verify color correspondence]. These panels extend the mixture model from numerical regression to spatial chemical imaging.

The distinction between Figures 4 and 5 is important. Figure 4 establishes predictive performance across controlled concentration conditions, whereas Figure 5 demonstrates spectral correction and application to spatially encoded mixtures. The model is therefore described as a calibrated inverse mapping within the measured domain. It cannot reconstruct a pesticide whose spectral evidence has been completely removed by surface competition, as observed in the high-concentration stress series.
